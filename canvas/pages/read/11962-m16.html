The Learning Cycle (for high school students)

Procedure:

1. Get the Lesson Content

Takes notes during a lesson (or use provided notes).

2. Do Homework

Complete homework from the lesson to improve your understanding of the content.

3. Study

  • Condense your knowledge. Summarise notes from the lesson and add it to a course notes document under a specified topic area. Ensure your notes are structured with a sub topic/question/learning intention followed by the required content/knowledge. A good method for this is the Cornell note taking structure. Feel free to do extra research to add more to your notes.
  • Practice applying your knowledge. Do practice questions or quizzes for the topic you are currently studying.

4. Revise

Come back to your notes at the end of the week (spaced repetition) and test if you can remember what you wrote under each sub topic (active recall). Reread parts you forgot.

5. Prepare for Exams

Before the exam, practice applying your knowledge to different types of questions, which is what the bulk of this Mastering the HSC Exam lesson is going to cover.

The different types of questions

It's important that you aware of and know how to answer all the different types of questions.

Why so many different types of questions?

Exams often have a variety of different questions types to make it less monotonous, test different skills, and accommodate different learning styles. Each question has the potential to test either your knowledge of the course content or your mastery of a particular skill. All the things they could test are included in your course syllabus, and further explanation of items where required in available in the course specifications.

Each types of question requires a different process to answer and it's important to develop effective strategies to maximise your marks in all question types. This module aims to equip you with the necessary understanding of how to approach each type of question from the perspective of both a student and marker.

NESA Key Verbs

NESA key verbs are usually the first word of a question and tells us exactly what we have to produce to get full marks. It defines the structure and is closely linked with the length of a response so it's crucial that you understand what they all mean and how to approach each verb. We will look at this is much more detail when we cover how to answer Short Answer questions.

Here are some common NESA key verbs you will encounter in your online exam. You can also view all the NESA key verbs if you encounter one not in the list below.

Verb Description
Identify Recognise and name.
Outline Sketch in general terms; indicate the main features of.
Describe Provide characteristics and features.
Compare Show how things are similar or different.
Discuss Identify issues and provide points for and/or against.
Explain
  • Relate cause and effect.
  • Make the relationships between things evident.
  • Provide why and/or how.
Recommend Provide reasons in favour.
Justify Support an argument or conclusion.

 

Common Mark Allocations & Expected Time Spent and Response Length

  • In the exam, you get roughly 1.8 minutes per mark, however you want to working slightly ahead of this if you can to ensure you have time for harder questions. This means you have about 5 minutes to complete a 3 mark question and about 9 minutes to complete a 5 mark question, etc. 
  • In the online exam environment there will be a word counter below of your answer text box and it will show something like "0/150 words", which shows you how many words you have written and how many words you are expected to write. Note that you are able to write more than the recommended number, there is no word limit. The word indicator will just start to show something like "183/150 words".
  • If you have the knowledge and time to write a little extra to include an "insurance policy" point, this is often a good idea so long as you stay within the time allocated for the question. However, it's not a good idea to write a lot less than the amount unless you are very succinct. A correctly structured response at the length specified is a recipe to get full marks.
Marks Max Time Allocation (1.8 mins per mark)  Time to aim for
1-2 1.8–3.6 mins 1-3 mins
3-4 5.4–7.2 mins 5-7 mins
5-6 9–10.8 mins 8-10 mins
7-8 12.6–14.4 mins 12-14 mins

Note that you should be aiming to complete question in slightly less time that in the table since some harder questions may require more time so you want to have a little time to spare at the end so you can go back to questions you flagged and develop more complex answers further. Use this table as a guide for the maximum time to spend on each question, but don't hesitate to work faster as long as you are confident. It's also important not to rush. Give each question it's due attention and love.

 

Important things to do as you answer questions

  • Relate to the scenario. A question worth more than two marks will almost always have some context or even a full stimulus which could be text, image(s), or even a video. To get full marks, you need to relate to the scenario, which involves tailoring what you write/create to the specific demands of the question, and including terms from the question in your response. Your response should be tailor made for the question so that it won't "fit" any other question. This shows you have a detailed understanding the content/skill and are able to mould your understanding to a specific context rather than regurgitate memorised information. The marker will notice this and it's often worth one mark explicitly in the marking criteria.
  • Give examples. Examples will add extra detail and clarity to your response, and are a powerful way to relate to the scenario. Even when there is no stimulus, the question will still have some context and you need to place yourself into that context and come up with examples to enhance your response and show a deeper understanding.
  • Put yourself in the shoes of a marker. Every question type on the following pages will have an example question with a marking criteria where you will learn to mark your own response and see how a marker assigns marks.
    • Knowing how a marker marks will help you know how to get full marks and also help you maximise your marks when you're not fully sure how to answer a question. Also, if you were the marker, think about how much nicer it is to mark if a student structures their response clearly and logically for the question.
    • Following the expected structure for a question, using text connectives between your points, and having a quick thesis that sets the scene for your answer all make it easier for the marker to pay you marks. You want to eliminate the friction for the marker handing out marks by giving them exactly what they want.
  • Use the correct structure. For short answer question the key verb will define how to structure your response and what to include. For other question types like drawing questions you just need to be on top of how to use the drawing tool to create what ever diagram they want. For other types of questions like multi-choice and fill-in the blanks, you just need to be familiar with how they work.
  • Identify the topic area. Knowing the topic area will make it far easier for you to recall the correct content for the question. You can get better at this while doing practice question by finding the syllabus dot point or part of the course specs that your question comes from. Your exam questions can only come from the syllabus or course specs. The course specs has some additional specification in addition to the syllabus and you should become very familiar with both since they will define the constrains on the questions you can expect to see. When you read the question, you should have a general idea of what topic the question is from, but open up your syllabus and try to find the exact dot point. The question examples on the following pages will identify the syllabus dot point and link you to the corresponding Canvas page to help you with this exercise. 
  • Know your content. After you have identified the topic area, you need to recall everything you know about it and flesh out your answer with as much content knowledge as you can feasibly cram in. You want your responses to be rich with detail and understanding.

In your exam you have a limited amount of time, so you need to formulate a plan of attack to maximise your performance. Here is a plan of attack to get you started.

The week before

Lifestyle

  • You need to stick to good eating and sleeping habits. To perform at your best, your body and mind must be healthy, fuelled, and engaged. This means eating proper, healthy meals and getting a suitable amount of sleep. You cannot rely on going to bed early the night before and thinking it will keep you awake the next day.
  • You shouldn't change your routine the day before an exam. An example could be deciding to drink a huge coffee on exam day. While this may sound like a good idea to keep yourself awake, if you're not used to coffee, you may find yourself needing to use the bathroom more than expected which takes time away form your exam.   • It then lets the user guess what the number is.

Step 3. Draw the flowchart

  1. We will start with the terminator symbol at the start with the BEGIN 'program name' inside it. In this case, the program is just called 'main'. 
  2. Next we need to get the user input which is technically a subroutine, but since we are also assigning the output of the subroutine to a variable, we will just put the assignment inside a process which will call the subroutine and assign it to the variable for us.

Based on whether this is true or false, we can draw arrows to different input output shapes with different messages. Since we are displaying this presumably on a screen, we will use the pseudocode DISPLAY keyword. 
  6. We want both of these displays to link back up to the 'get user guess' stage so the game can continue.
  7. Now we just need to draw arrows between all the shapes to show the logic of the game. We draw the arrows last because it makes it easier to move the shapes around as we tweak the logic and aesthetics.

Question: Draw the decision tree to distinguish between the web protocols. (3 marks)

Step 1. Read the question carefully

When reading the question, it's important to ask these two questions:

  1. What is the topic/content area? This question is testing our ability to create a decision tree in the context of web protocols. Decision trees can be found on page 10 of your course specifications and web protocols are in your Programming for the Web topic under "Investigate and describe the function of web protocols and their ports". Revise 2.3 Web Protocols for more information.
  2. What are we required to do? We need to draw a decision tree that allows us to distinguish between web protocols. We will need to refer to the stimulus for the exact requirements.

Stimulus:

A student is interested in testing their knowledge of web protocols so they create a decision tree that can identify a protocol based on it’s characteristics.

The decision tree needs to allow someone distinguish between HTTP, HTTPS, POP, SMTP, and IMAP. It should ask appropriate yes/no questions about the protocol’s characteristics to arrive at a unique conclusion.

For example if a protocol is used for transferring web pages on the internet and runs on port 80, then it is HTTP. This could be one of the paths in the decision tree, but it also needs to account for the other protocols by asking appropriate questions and including appropriate pathways that end at a single protocol.

Step 2. Analyse the stimulus

The stimulus makes it clear that we need to make a decision tree that asks increasingly nuanced questions that allow us to differentiate between the five protocols mentioned. We also need to arrive at a unique conclusion, so we can have one branch that says it could be HTTP or HTTPS, we need to have a single protocol at the end of each pathway. Also, we can only ask yes/no questions so we can't just have one level with five branches that ask what port the protocol runs on and distinguish the protocols like that. Instead, we need to ask four yes no questions to adequately tell the five protocols apart.

For each question as we move down the tree, we need to think of a new more targeted characteristic that can allow us to separate the protocols from each other sort of like how we would use a decision tree to diagnose an illness — first we start broad like where the pain is or what the protocol is used for and get finer such as what type of pain is felt or what port the protocol runs on.

Step 3. Draw the decision tree

  1. Since we need to ask at least three questions before arriving at a conclusion, we will ask a broad question to begin with like 'what is the protocol used for'. To make this a yes or no question, we will use "Used for transferring webpage data." If this question is yes it could be either HTTP or HTTPS and if not, it could be SMTP, IMAP, or POP3.
  2. Now to differentiate between HTTP and HTTPS the we could use the port it runs on. If it runs on port 80 it is HTTPS and if not, it is HTTPS. We could also ask a question of if it is encrypted or not which is another valid characteristic. Now we have finished this side of the tree.
  3. Now we need to start differentiating between SMTP, IMAP, and POP3. To do this we need to find a characteristic that only one of the three has. In this case we will ask if the protocol is used to transfer mail between mail servers since only SMTP is used for this.
  4. Now we are only left with the branch that deals with IMAP and POP3. To distinguish between these we need another appropriate characteristic. We know that only IMAP is used to synchronise mail across devices while POP3 simply retrieves mail from the server to one device. So, we can ask weather the protocol is used to synchronise email across devices and if yes, it is IMAP and if no, it's POP3.

Step 4. Examine your work and make sure it's correct

Looking back to our highlights of the stimulus we have only used yes/no questions, all our branches end at single protocols, and all the questions use defining features of the protocols to accurately distinguish them. Therefore we can be fairly confident our answer is correct.

Question: Write the algorithm using pseudocode. Include at least one subroutine in your answer. (4 marks)
The array is called day_array and it is indexed from 1.

Step 1. Read the question carefully

When reading the question, it's important to ask these two questions:

  1. What is the topic/content area? This question is testing our ability to create algorithms and present them as pseudocode.
  2. What are we required to do? We are required to read the stimulus and write the algorithm described. We must ensure that we include at least one subroutine. We also get some crucial information regarding the name of the array we will be using and that it is indexed from 1.

Stimulus:

A company is developing a learning management system and wants to build an algorithm that will help schedule a student’s homework time to improve productivity.

A student’s day is represented as an array of records with a length of 48. This account for all the 30 minute time slots of a given day. Each record has a state which can be occupied or free. Slots that are free are empty and slots that are occupied have an ‘X’. The record also has a slot type which could be ‘H’ for homework, ‘S’ for school time, and ‘F’ for free time. Homework time can only be added to time slots that have a type “H”.

Your job is to write an algorithm that takes the number of homework slots a student would like to add to their schedule and see if it fits in their current day. If there is enough space, each homework time slot that is used should be filled with an ‘X”. The program should then return “Homework Added”.

If there is not enough space, it should return “Not enough space” followed by the number of extra homework slots required. E.g “Not enough space. 1 extra homework slot required”.

For example, if the array looked like the one below and the student wanted to add one homework slot, the program would add an X to the availability part of the record at index 32 and return “Homework Added”.


Step 2. Analyse the stimulus

You need to highlight everything you are told about the nature of the program. In this case everything we know about the array which stores the students daily schedule is highlighted in yellow.

Highlight what you need to make (in green above) and then all the requirements of the program (in blue above).

Array based question will sometimes include a diagram for you to aid in understanding the scenario.

Note that since we are working with an array of records, we can access each field of the record using the dot operator like day_array[i].type

Step 3. Write your mainline and decide on the subroutines you will use.

Figuring out the mainline will come primarily from our blue highlights since they define the different outputs and pathways our algorithm needs to have. We will also identify the subroutines we need as we write the mainline.

  1. The first thing the program needs to do is check the availability for adding a certain number of homework slots. We will use a check_space subroutine to see if there is space. This subroutine will return 0 if there is space and if there isn't, it will return the number of extra spaces required.
  2. Then if there is space, we need to add the set the slots of occupied and return "Homework Added". We will use a subroutine called assign_homework to set the homework slots of occupied.
  3. If there is no space, we send a message to the user with the number of extra slots required to meet the request.

These three requirements give us the following mainline.

BEGIN add_homework (slots)
        is_space = check_space (slots)
        IF is_space = 0 THEN
                assign_homework (slots)
                DISPLAY 'Homework Added'
        ELSE
                DISPLAY 'Not enough space. ' + is_space + 'extra homework slots required'.
        ENDIF
END add_homework (slots)

Note

The mainline is very simple, short, and un-cluttered. This is expected by the markers. You should not have your complicated, low-level logic in the mainline. The mainline should only contain a high-level overview that anyone could understand after reading it once. The subroutines it calls should do all the complex work.

Step 4. Write your subroutines.

 In step 3, we identified two subroutines. These were check_space and assign_homework.

check_space requires us to iterate over all the time slots of the day and count how many free homework slots we have. To keep track of the free homework slots we use a variable called slots_free and we increment it when we find an index of the array that is not occupied and has a type of 'H' for homework. We are using a while loop because we don't just want to blindly iterate over the entire array, but rather keep going until we find the required number. If we get to the end and still don't have the required number, then we can confirm there are not enough slots, but if we find enough early, we don't want to keep iterating over the rest of the array since that would be inefficient. 

assign_homework is used to set the availability of a certain number of homework slots being added by the user. Since we have already confirmed there are enough slots from the check_space subroutine, we can iterate over the array setting the availability of each homework slot to 'X' for however many we are adding.

Step 5. Reread the question and test the algorithm.

  1. Check  your pseudocode syntax. You need to make sure the every IF finishes with an ENDIF and every WHILE end with and ENDWHILE. Recall that FOR loops don't have a standard ending keyword, but instead uses NEXT variable after the code inside the loop. Also ensure each of your subroutines have an END subroutine_name (all parameters if any) including all the parameters it takes in, if any. Make sure all these key works are capitalised.
  2. Check for silly mistakes. Ensure that you haven't forgotten to increment any variables your are using for iteration. E.g don't forget to iterate count or slots_free in this question. 
  3. Check your logic. Test your subroutine in your head by passing in a value and running through it step by step to see what output you get. Make sure this is the correct output. If you subroutine has lots of cases, try values that lie on the edges of the cases to ensure all the pathways work as you expect.

Justify Questions

NESA Definition — Justify

Support an argument or conclusion.

Justify questions require you to provide evidence or an explanation that supports an argument or conclusion. It's sort of like a mini essay where you have a thesis, which is the conclusion you are required to support, and you need to come up with all the evidence in the body of the essay to support that thesis.

Answer Structure

  1. Topic sentence or phrase that addresses the question explicitly and sets up the thesis you are supporting.
  2. Body of your response that provides evidence which supports the argument or conclusion.

Question: Recommend and justify a suitable development approach for the e-commerce mobile application.

Step 1. Read the Question Carefully.

When reading a question, it's important to ask these two questions:

  1. What is the topic/content area? This question is testing your knowledge of development approaches. In your syllabus, this is under the "Explore and apply the most suitable development approach to develop, modify and implement an enterprise system" dot point of Enterprise project. If you've forgotten this, revise 4.7 Development Approach.
  2. What are we required to do? Highlight the NESA key verb in red so you don't forget it. We are required to recommend and justify a suitable development approach for the e-commerce mobile application in the scenario. This means we need to identify a suitable development approach, and then we need to provide evidence for why it is a good pick. Note, this is more of a skills application question since you are not regurgitating information, but rather applying your analysis and justification skills to make and support and judgement based on an unseen scenario. This makes it harder than some of the earlier questions we looked at and may take some extra time.

Stimulus:

A software company has been contracted to develop a mobile application for a new e-commerce business.

The application must be delivered within six months to meet the client’s launch date. It must include key features such as secure user authentication, a shopping cart, and payment gateway integration.

The solution has to have the ability to scale as the business grows. The client doesn't have a large budget, so they would like the keep costs relatively low.

Step 2. Analyse the stimulus.

Since we know we are answering a development approach question, we should recall all the development approaches we know before we read the stimulus. These are waterfall (structured), agile, prototyping, end user, and outsourcing. If you struggle with these questions, write down each of these approaches in the answer text box and then you can delete the ones that will be too hard to justify as you read the stimulus.

In the first line, the general premise of the stimulus have been set. We need to put ourselves in the shoes of a software company, that has been contracted to develop a mobile application for a new e-commerce business. The end user approach is immediately out since the e-commerce business has hired our software agency rather than doing it themselves. Now, mobile applications, especially for e-commerce businesses are generally medium quality products meaning they have to be robust, but not to the same level of rigour as an air traffic system at an airpot. This means that the agile development approach is already a decent option and the traditional waterfall approach is probably harder, but we won't eliminate it yet.

As we keep reading, we see that the product needs to be "delivered within six months", so this makes it even harder to justify the waterfall approach. The next key phrase we see is "key features" which points us further in the direction of agile which focuses of delivering high priority features iteratively to produce a MVP at a relatively fast pace. Right now, agile is looking pretty good, but prototyping and outsourcing are still open (though, harder to justify with the current evidence given their characteristics). However, the next key phrase, "scale as the business grows" makes them both less viable and agile a better option since agile has the flexible to shift and grow iteratively. Finally, the client "doesn't have a large budget" means waterfall is super hard to justify now, and agile has a lot more going for it now.

Based on what we know about each development approach, we can easily recommend Agile, but now we need to produce the evidence that supports that recommendation.

Step 3. Plan your response quickly in your head.

For a recommend and justify question, we just need to have a topic sentence that introduces the item we are recommending and then a body which provides reasons and evidence for why our recommendation stands in relation to the stimulus and question.

Our recommendation will be the Agile approach.

Our evidence will be:

  • Prioritisation of a MVP to quickly deliver a product with key features.
  • Flexibility of agile to accomodate evolving future need.
  • Small, skilled dev team minimises costs for a limited budget.

After each piece of evidence, we need to explicitly link it back to how it supports the agile development approach.

Step 4. Write your response

  • Follow the right structure for a recommend and justify question:
    • Start with a topic sentence which addresses the terms of the question and clearly introduces the thing you are supporting. In this case we are supporting a recommendation for a development approach.
    • Use text connectives between your pieces of evidence to segment them for the marker. Good text connectives are "furthermore", "additionally", and "moreover".
    • After each piece of evidence, we need to explicitly link it back to how it supports the agile development approach.
  • Relate to the Scenario/Question. Never forget the golden rule "relate to the scenario/question". This means you should use terms from the question throughout your response make links back to the ideas/context given in the question. In this case, relating to the scenario, will involve mentioning the e-commerce business and their requirements from the stimulus and how Agile's characteristics are a good fit for their needs. Below, the links back to the question have been highlighted in purple. 
  • Give clear examples. When there is no explicit scenario, it's important to give examples to enhance the clarity and nuance of answer. In this describe question, giving examples is an extremely powerful way to elevate your description and prove to the marker you know what you are talking about.
  • Know your content. It's important to think about where this question comes from in your syllabus as you read the question, recall what you have learned about that topic and then include as much relevant information as you can to flesh out your answer.

Sample Answer Analysis: The Agile development approach is the most suitable for developing the e-commerce mobile application. Agile is centred around iterative development in scrum cycles, allowing for rapid delivery of a minimum viable product (MVP) within the six-month deadline. This ensures that essential features, such as secure authentication and payment integration, can be prioritised and delivered early in the project. Furthermore, the flexibility of Agile also supports future scalability by enabling continuous updates and improvements in response to the business's growth and evolving user needs. Additionally, Agile minimises costs by focusing on high-priority features in each iteration, reducing unnecessary development work.

Step 5. Check your response is correct

Quickly reread the question and try highlighting your response in your head if you're in an exam or in a word processor if you are practicing. Make sure there is enough purple and blue and also some green and yellow.

  1. We have structured our response appropriately for a justify question. Here we have clearly identified the thing we are supporting and provided three strong pieces of evidence to justify the recommendation.
  2. We've related to the scenario. We've also included terms from the question and our pieces of evidence explicitly link to the stimulus and question.

  • Structure your response appropriately. A justify question needs to have clear pieces of evidence that support your thesis in the topic sentence. In this case we need to have clear evidence in the body that supports the use of the Agile development approach. Assess

Assess Questions

NESA Definition — Assess

Make a judgement of value, quality, outcomes, results or size.

These assess questions tend to be worth more marks (5+) and are typically located near the end of your exam. They usually require a mini essay like structure with a short introduction and then paragraphs delving into your points and finally, a summarising conclusion at the end.

Answer Structure

  1. A topic sentence or introduction paragraph that introduces your judgement of value, quality, outcomes, results or size.
  2. The body of your response is usually a few paragraphs that supports the judgement you made in your introduction by exploring different points and how they fit together. Each sentence you write should be form the mindset of evaluating the value, quality, outcome, result or size of something and how this supports your overall judgement in the response.
  3. A conclusion that summarises everything is optional, but a good thing for completeness especially for questions more than 6 marks.

NESA Definition — Analyse

  • Identify components and the relationship between them.

  2. A body that explains each of the smaller components, their implications and how the relate.
  3. A conclusion that summarises your ideas and restates the final implication of the analysis.
