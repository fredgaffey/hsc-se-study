  Content

When building mechatronic systems, data is gathered within the system and from the environment to regulate the system and maintain it, so as to make sure the system achieves its purpose in an efficient manner.

 

Sensor Data

Types of Sensors

Data in mechatronic systems are given from sensors connected to the microcontroller. Sensors can either be analog or digital, where analog sensors give a range of values whereas digital sensors give a boolean value (HIGH or LOW, which is the value of VCC and GND). Analog sensors include potentiometers (dials), temperature sensors, pressure sensors, light sensors. Digital sensors include digital buttons such as light switches, keyboard switches, or sensors such as knock sensors.

Arduino pin diagram. VCC refers to control power which will be +5V or +3V3.
From: https://docs.arduino.cc/retired/boards/arduino-uno-rev3-with-long-pins/

Sensor Construction

Analog sensors are typically connected with at least three wires, one to ground pin, one to power, and one to data. To get a reading from the sensor, the sensor sends a current at a specific voltage to the data pin, allowing the microcontroller to convert the voltage reading to a value. The ground and power pins are used as LOW and HIGH references respectively as a reference for the voltage reading given by the sensor.

Digital sensors such as buttons typically include only two pins which are typically connected to GND and data pins. When pressing the button with solely those wires, the voltage reading at the data pin is floating, meaning it is undefined. To get a solid reading there needs to be a pullup or pulldown resistor connected to the wire, which is connected to VCC.

From: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potentiometer#/media/File:Electronic-Component-Potentiometer.jpg

ADC

In the case of an analog sensor, the voltage signal needs to be converted into a digital value by an analog to digital converter (ADC), as digital computers can only manipulate discrete data and not the analog continuous voltage signal. The ADC samples the analog input at normal intervals to build a discrete snapshot of the continuous data, which converts the continuous signal into a digital bit format at each time interval. This would be similar to a histogram.

From: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analog-to-digital_converter#/media/File:Conversion_AD_DA.png

With digital sensors, they will only give HIGH or LOW voltage readings, which is the value of power and ground respectively, and this will be read by the microcontroller pin which will read the signal as LOW unless it passes a set voltage threshold to be HIGH. The reason is that sometimes the signal will have voltage drops so the sensor will need to account for this.

 

 

Protocol Bus (I2C)

Oftentimes we may want to use multiple peripherals despite only having a limited number of pins. To use multiple peripherals while only using two wires, the I2C protocol bus can be used. I2C functions by having the master controller - typically the microcontroller - request for a read or write from a specific peripheral based on its address, then having data sent across the bus. Data is sent across the bus through the SDA (Serial Data) pin bit by bit, which is synced by a clock signal sent by the master device on SCL (Serial Clock). This means that a bit is sent whenever the clock signal is at HIGH or LOW.

An I2C message starts with the master sending the 7 bit address of the peripheral across the bus which tells it which peripheral to be read or written to. Then it sends 1 bit to signify a read or write operation. The peripheral then sends an acknowledgement bit to acknowledge that the information has been received. Then data is sent in 8 bits from either the controller or peripheral, and then the receiver sends an acknowledgement bit to receive the next chunk of data.

From: https://docs.arduino.cc/learn/communication/wire/

 

 

Controlling actuators and PWM signals

Data is used to control actuators such as DC motors. Since microcontrollers control signals and motors require large amounts of current, motor drivers with external power sources not from the microcontroller are used to power motors.

For actuators with digital input, they are typically controlled by sending a pulse width modulated (PWM) signal. A PWM signal is a digital signal that sends data by encoding the data as the period of HIGH pulses within the signal. The percentage of time the signal is on is called the duty cycle, so a signal that is always on has a duty cycle of 100% which would be full power, and a signal that is always off has a duty cycle of 0% which would be grounded. To control an LED to be at half power, the microcontroller can send a PWM signal at a duty cycle of 50%. For the LED, the frequency of the signal should be fast enough to be invisible to the eye.

From: https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/pulse-width-modulation/all

Mechatronic systems also typically use servo motors. Servo motors, unlike DC motors, do not have speed, but instead have position as they rotate their axle from one specific angle to another specific angle. Most servo motors expect an update every 20ms with a pulse between 1 to 2ms, i.e. a 5% to 10% duty cycle. At a 5% duty cycle the servo motor will be at 0 degrees and at 10% it will be at 180 degrees, thus a pulse at 1.5ms will rotate the motor to be at 90 degrees. Therefore to rotate the servo motor at a specific position in between, the microcontroller must send a signal between 1 and 2ms.

 

Feedback Loop

Mechatronic systems are typically driven in a continuous feedback loop that takes in environmental data gathered by sensors to ensure optimal usage of actuators. A commonly used controller for these systems is PID (proportional, integral, derivative). For example, a line follow wheeled robot is tasked with following a black line on the floor, so its sensor picks up the deviation from the line which then tells the motors how to move. Once the robot moves, this then affects the readings taken by the sensor. Therefore the system must optimise by moving the motors in a path that reduces the deviance from the line. To minimise deviance, we can use the PID controller which attempts to minimise deviance from a target by moving the actuators in accordance to three terms.

 

1. Proportional: Term that corrects for current error

2. Integral: Term that corrects for past error

3. Derivative: Term that corrects for the error's rate of change

 

In real world applications, PID is used to automatically correct errors, sometimes this will be error along a path, such as in car cruise controls, or controlled variables such as temperature by setting a target temperature.

Diagram of typical PID system. System corrects for the initial error, which causes an overshoot which is correcting, creating an oscillating pattern until it settles on the target.
