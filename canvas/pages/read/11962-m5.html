1.21 Development approaches

Syllabus
Compare the execution of the Waterfall and Agile project management models as applied to software development.

  Learning Intention and Success Criteria

Learning Intention - Students will:

  • Compare the Waterfall and Agile project management models as applied to software development.

Success Criteria - Students can:

  • Define the Waterfall and Agile project management models.
  • Understand the differences between the Waterfall and Agile project management models.
  • Explain the advantages and disadvantages of each model.
  • Identify the types of projects that are suitable for each model.

  Content

Waterfall Model

Sequential Phases: The Waterfall model follows a linear and sequential approach where the project is divided into distinct phases. Each phase depends on the deliverables of the previous one and corresponds to a specialisation of tasks.

Documentation: There is a heavy emphasis on documentation at each phase. Before proceeding to the next phase, documentation must be completed and approved, ensuring that each stage's requirements are thoroughly understood and met.

Planning and Predictability: Detailed planning is crucial to the Waterfall model. The scope, budget, timeline, resources, and testing plans are defined early in the project. This approach aims for predictability and minimal changes during the project lifecycle.

Testing: In the Waterfall model, testing is a distinct phase that occurs after development is completed. Any errors discovered during testing can lead to significant revisions in previous work.

Client Involvement: Client involvement is generally limited to the requirements phase and final approval. After the project has moved beyond the initial stages, changes can be challenging to implement and often require returning to the drawing board.

 

The waterfall model is particularly suited to software development, where all requirements can be defined and understood before planning and design commence. It is most advantageous for custom large-scale systems that have precise specifications. Most errors occur in the requirements definition and formation of the software's specifications, with results showing that more than half of all errors occur at this stage. Correcting mistakes becomes increasingly expensive as the development cycle progresses.

 

Agile Model

Iterative Development: Unlike Waterfall, Agile adopts an iterative and incremental approach. Development is conducted in cycles (sprints), allowing for frequent reassessment and adaptation of plans. This approach facilitates flexibility in responding to changes.

The stages in an agile model are generally shorter than those in a waterfall model. These stages include:

  1. Defining and understanding
  2. Planning, designing and implementing
  3. Testing and evaluation
  4. Release a working version
  5. Define new requirements and repeat from the 2nd stage

Continuous Collaboration: Agile emphasises constant collaboration among team members and with stakeholders. Daily stand-ups, sprint reviews, and retrospectives are standard practices that encourage ongoing communication and adjustment.

Adaptability: Agile is highly adaptive, allowing for project scope and requirements changes even late in the development process. This flexibility is seen as a strength, enabling the delivery of a product that better meets the user's needs.

Testing: In Agile, testing is integrated throughout the development process. Continuous integration and delivery practices ensure that testing is ongoing, allowing for the early detection of issues and smoother iterations.

Client Involvement: Clients or end-users are significantly more involved throughout the project. Their feedback is incorporated into each iteration, ensuring the final product is closely aligned with their expectations and needs.

 

The agile model is a recently emerged development method responding to software development projects' "ad-hoc" reality. It emphasises the team developing the system rather than the predefined structured steps. The agile model removes the need for detailed requirements and complex design documentation; rather, it encourages cooperation, communication, and teamwork between the developers and the client. It is well suited to web-based software development and software that requires regular modification as it evolves and updates.

Small teams of developers are typically used, not much more than half a dozen developers. Small teams can share ideas better and work on solutions together. Larger teams tend to break into smaller groups. For the agile model to succeed, everyone must be an equal member with a clear shared purpose. Team members are often multi-skilled to contribute to multiple development activities.

The agile model allows a working version of the software to be released in a shorter amount of time. Basic functionality should be the initial version so the software can be released immediately. Ongoing interactions between the team and the client allow the solution to be refined throughout development. New versions are regularly released, adding the next most important features or adapting to changing specifications.

Phases Divided into distinct, sequential phases Shorter stages with cycles (sprints)
Documentation Heavy emphasis on documentation at each phase Emphasis on collaboration over extensive documentation
Planning and Predictability Detailed planning early in the project for predictability Adaptive planning, allows for changes even late in the process
Testing Testing as a distinct phase after development completion Testing integrated throughout the development process
Client Involvement Limited to requirements phase and final approval Continuous involvement; client feedback incorporated iteratively
Flexibility to Changes Changes challenging to implement once project progresses Highly adaptive, accommodates changes throughout

Development Speed Slower due to sequential nature Faster release of working versions, enabling quick iterations

Scenario 1: Construction Project

  • Description: A company is developing a large-scale construction project management software that requires strict regulatory compliance and integration with legacy systems. The requirements are well-defined, and the project scope is clear, with no expected changes.
  • Why Waterfall: The Waterfall model is ideal for this scenario due to the project's complexity and the need for extensive documentation and planning. Each phase (requirements, design, implementation, etc.) can be meticulously executed with approvals at every stage, ensuring compliance and compatibility. The sequential nature of Waterfall fits projects where changes are costly, and requirements are not expected to change.

 

Scenario 2: E-Commerce Mobile Application

  • Description: A startup wants to develop an e-commerce mobile application to enter the market quickly. The initial release will be followed by regular updates based on user feedback and emerging market trends.
  • Why Agile: Agile is suitable for this scenario because it allows the project to adapt rapidly to changing requirements and user feedback. The iterative development process enables the team to release a minimum viable product (MVP) quickly and refine it over time based on actual user interactions. Agile facilitates continuous improvement and can effectively respond to competitors and market demands.
